exact_construct: "Fragile tonic (present but hierarchically weakened by inversion or mid-phrase placement); emergent tonic (withheld until later arrival, usually chorus); absent tonic (promised tonic never materializes)."
task_repertoire: "Close readings of selected pop/rock songs 1960s–2010s (Hall & Oates, Four Tops, Elton John, Prince, Jane's Addiction, Spinners, Human League, Fleetwood Mac, Psychedelic Furs, Coldplay, etc.). Analyst's hearing; NOT an experiment, NOT a corpus study (author explicitly disavows systematic corpus method, §10)."
what_supports: "Tonic chord can be present yet weakened (first/second inversion; mid-phrase instead of structural departure/arrival). Tonic can be deliberately withheld and arrive later (emergent). Progressions can promise a tonic that never arrives (absent). Short circular progressions are often ambiguous major vs. relative minor. Two-chord vamp interpretation depends on durational accent, bass pedal, emphasized melody pitches/riffs, and chord order (§12). Melody can establish key while harmony withholds tonic (cites Smith 2016). Relative major/minor competition and double-tonic complexes documented (cites Krebs 1981; Capuzzo 2009; Osborn 2016 on Radiohead 'Reckoner')."
what_does_not_support: "No perceptual measurement of tonic attribution; no prevalence/frequency statistics; no compositional recipe for producing ambiguity (repertoire exhibits ambiguity, does not test a cue-distribution method)."
important_limitations:
  - Repertoire analysis from the author's hearing and 'database in the head'; analyst-relative judgments (author acknowledges controversial readings, e.g. fn17).
  - No listener data; cannot quantify attribution or cue weights.
  - Do NOT cite for 'distribute cues to create ambiguity' as a tested method.
